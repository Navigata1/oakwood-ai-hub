Capability Model

Four pillars. One studio.

Every student moves through the same four capabilities — fluency, building, local infrastructure, and responsible governance. The lab makes each one a daily practice instead of an abstract topic. Graduates leave with proof: shipped projects, public demos, GitHub portfolios, and the judgment to deploy AI well.

Use

Use AI with skill and integrity.

Practical prompting, research, analysis, design, coding, and presentation. The fluency every CS graduate now needs from week one.

PILLAR I · FLUENCY
Build

Build apps, agents, and automations.

Real applications: RAG systems, voice interfaces, dashboards, campus tools. From idea to deploy on Vercel and Cloudflare in a week.

PILLAR II · CREATION
Run

Run frontier models locally.

Hands-on with Ollama, LM Studio, vLLM, quantization, and 70B–200B-parameter inference. Understanding the tradeoffs, not just the API.

Govern

Govern AI as a core competency.

Red-teaming, evaluation, prompt-injection defense, bias testing, policy drafting. The skills internships and federal employers now ask for by name.

02
The Space

A room that signals "AI production" from the door.

Not a traditional computer lab with rows of identical screens. A studio zoned for iteration, collaboration, and showcase — so the work students do is visible from the hallway, on the demo wall, and in the videos they publish.

A.

Student Build Zone

Ten seats arranged for pair-programming and code review. Every workstation is a capable AI machine — not a thin client to the cloud.

B.

Local Model Bench

Shared high-memory nodes for running frontier open models locally. Where students learn quantization, latency, privacy tradeoffs — the real engineering.

C.

Demo Wall

A large display for live builds, evaluations, weekly showcases, and visitor presentations. The wall earns its rent every Friday.

D.

Studio Corner

Camera, lighting, mics. Students publish their demos. Portfolio quality lives or dies at this corner.

E.

Edge AI Bench

Jetson devices, vision sensors, robotics kits. Where AI becomes physical — and where curiosity finds a hardware home.

F.

Governance Table

The room reserved for evaluation harnesses, red-team exercises, policy drafts, and audit logs. Responsibility lives in furniture, not footnotes.

04
Architecture

How the pieces connect.

Three layers, one network. Student seats for daily creation. Shared local nodes for the heavy work. Edge devices for physical AI. Everything routes through a fast internal network with proper storage, governance, and the demo wall as a first-class output.

SHARED LOCAL AI NODE — DGX SPARK

Memory128 GB unified LPDDR5X
AI compute1 PFLOP FP4
Model ceiling200B params (4-bit, single node)
Pair-linked256 GB / 405B params via ConnectX-7

Why local matters. Privacy-sensitive student work — campus data, research datasets, voice — never leaves the room. Inference cost stays predictable. And students learn the actual engineering of running models, not just calling someone else's endpoint.

07
Governance

Responsibility is the superpower, not the warning label.

Every employer hiring AI talent right now is asking the same second question after "can you build it?" — "can you evaluate it, defend it, and document it?" The lab makes responsible AI the daily habit, not an end-of-semester slide.

  • α
    Data classification, by default

    Every dataset gets labeled before it touches a model. Students learn the discipline employers expect.

  • β
    Human approval gates

    Sensitive actions — sending email, writing to a database, financial transactions — always pass through a person.

  • γ
    Habitual evaluation

    Hallucination tests, bias checks, prompt-injection defense, quality metrics — built into every project, not bolted on.

  • δ
    Policy as artifact

    Students draft real usage policies, audit trails, and incident playbooks. The artifacts transfer directly to internships.

Why this matters at an HBCU

Oakwood's graduates will work in environments where AI decisions affect their own communities — health, finance, criminal justice, education, civic systems. Teaching governance as a craft means producing builders who understand the weight of what they're building. That's not a warning. It's a competitive advantage and a moral one.

The lab makes Oakwood a place that produces the people federal agencies, healthcare systems, and Huntsville's defense and biotech ecosystem are looking for right now — and not finding enough of.
